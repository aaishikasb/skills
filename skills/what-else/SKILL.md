# What else?

Turn the itch to keep improving into a considered next move. Look beyond the artifact to who uses it, how it reaches them, and what keeping it useful will take.

## Establish what exists

Read the available project, outcomes, feedback, previous decisions, and unfinished work. Identify the objective, audience, stage, commitments, and constraints. Check whether a proposed addition already exists or was deliberately excluded. Reference files, sections, observations, or sources; distinguish missing evidence from a demonstrated gap.

Use the supplied time, budget, skills, energy, collaborators, and deadline. If a missing fact would change the recommendation, ask a focused question while completing the review that is possible. Otherwise state assumptions and give a provisional table. Unknown capacity is not unlimited capacity. Match the ambition to the user's goal: learning, craft, reach, and exploration can be legitimate outcomes alongside adoption or revenue.

## Look across domains

Consider the applicable dimensions: usefulness and experience; correctness and reliability; explanation and teaching; accessibility and inclusion; discovery and distribution; community and feedback; measurement and learning; operations and maintenance. Include physical constraints, safety, cost, or commercialization when the project makes them relevant. Adapt these lenses to software, content, events, hardware, research, or other work; no domain needs a token recommendation.

Look for connections: a support question might warrant a clearer example rather than another feature; an existing demo might become a workshop; removing a fragile dependency might free time for outreach. Consider fixes, subtraction, reuse, validation, and distribution as well as additions. Include a promising stretch opportunity when there is a credible connection to the goal, labeled as a bet with a small way to test it.

Research consequential external claims when they affect the choice, using current primary sources. Bound comparisons to what was inspected. With inaccessible sources or limited evidence, name the uncertainty and the smallest check that would resolve it. Do not invent demand, novelty, guaranteed returns, or measurements to make a ranking look rigorous.

## Make the tradeoffs visible

Keep these judgments separate:

- **Impact:** what improves, for whom, and why that matters to the objective. State expected benefit and confidence, with the supporting evidence.
- **Urgency:** the real deadline, dependency, or cost of waiting. High impact alone does not make something urgent; “no deadline evidenced” is valid.
- **Need:** required for a stated promise or constraint, useful for the goal, optional exploration, or unproven. Explain what makes a requirement necessary.
- **Bandwidth:** estimated active effort by owner or role, plus consequential waiting time, dependencies, cash/equipment, and recurring upkeep. Use rough ranges or “needs scoping”; label estimates and distinguish effort from the owner's available capacity.
- **Priority:** recommend now, next, later, or skip, based on the above. Prefer resolving consequential uncertainty before committing to a large build. Use numeric scoring only when requested or supported by comparable inputs; expose assumptions.

## Deliver a decision table

Open with the most useful next move and the objective it serves. State the review's scope and any assumptions briefly, then use this table:

| Priority | Next move / domain | Impact | Urgency | Need | Bandwidth | Evidence / uncertainty |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Keep rows specific and independently actionable. Usually five to eight candidates are enough; use fewer when warranted. Order by recommended sequence, accounting for dependencies. Explain any important tradeoff the labels alone conceal. Briefly name relevant areas checked where no action is justified, rather than filling the table with speculative work.

End with a capacity-aware choice: the selected next step, its observable success signal, and what to defer. If capacity is known, total the selected effort ranges, allow for uncertainty, and show that the proposed commitment fits. If required work exceeds capacity, surface the conflict and a scope or timing decision. If capacity is unknown, offer a bounded first check without claiming the wider plan fits. Shipping, observing, or keeping the project as it is may be the best choice; give a revisit trigger rather than manufacturing additions.

The review is complete when the user can choose what to pursue and what to leave alone. Recommendations do not themselves authorize implementation, purchases, publication, or outreach; continue only within the user's requested scope.
